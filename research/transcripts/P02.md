## Analyst notes

Written after receipt, before coding against the schema.

**Q2 replicates P01 exactly, and that is the important thing.** Independently, on a different app,
a second respondent named baby care as the never-bought category and gave the same reason: *"I don't
have kids."* No competing channel was named, because there is no purchase happening anywhere. The
question asks "where do you buy that instead" and both respondents have declined to answer it,
because the premise doesn't hold.

Two of two is not a coincidence in a way one of one could have been. The `barrier[]` enum in
`engine/schema.py` still has **no code for "this category does not apply to me"** — every one of its
eight values presumes latent demand that something is obstructing. The survey's closed list would
have forced both P01 and P02 into a barrier that misdescribes them, and the discovery engine cannot
detect the gap because reviews never state an absence of need. This is now the clearest thing the
interviews have contributed that no other source in the project could.

Note the tail of the answer though: *"in future I can try"* — P01 said *"if I have in future I would
love to try."* Both frame it as deferred-until-relevant rather than rejected. The category isn't
blocked; it's dormant pending a life event. That distinction matters for the MVP, because a card
promoting baby care to either of these users would be wasted impressions, not a conversion
opportunity — and dismissal rate is a guardrail metric.

**Q5 contradicts P01. First real divergence between respondents.**

- P01: *"I don't shop everyday, I shop when I needed the products."*
- P02: *"I shop mostly regularly because it's very easy to shop on app."*

This is worth flagging rather than smoothing over, because the root cause in
`docs/03-problem-definition.md` §2 rests on a **two-minute fetch session** — the user opens the app
with the product already decided, so evaluating an unfamiliar category costs time they have not
budgeted. P02 self-describes as a regular shopper, which on its face weakens that model.

But P02's own Q1 and Q4 cut against their Q5. The last order was *cigarettes at late night because
it was available*; the last new-category purchase was *liquid detergent, urgent, no shop around*.
Both are single-item, need-triggered, availability-driven fetches. What P02 appears to mean by
"regularly" is **frequency**, not **browsing**. High order frequency and narrow fetch behaviour are
not in tension — they are precisely the "Habitual Narrow Repeaters" segment definition in
`docs/00-foundation.md` (≥3 orders/month, ≥70% spend in ≤2 categories). Frequent *and* narrow.

I am recording this as a **partial challenge** rather than a confirmation, because the reconciliation
is my inference from their other answers, not something P02 said. If a later respondent describes
actually browsing, the fetch-mode model is in real trouble and Part 3 has to be rewritten.

**Q4 replicates the urgency mechanism, and adds a second condition.** P01's crossover (refined oil)
was pure urgency. P02's has three parts stated in order: urgent → no shop nearby → *"and pricing is
good too."* Price appears as a **confirming** factor after the trigger, not as the trigger itself.
That ordering is consistent with the MVP's anchor design — price context is what closes a
consideration that something else opened, which is why the agent shows an anchor line only when it
already has a substantiated reason.

**Q3 names the same two things as P01, in the same rough weighting.** P01: quality, quantity,
pricing. P02: pricing, quality. Both name price and quality unprompted; neither mentions delivery
speed, returns, or brand — the three things quick-commerce marketing tends to lead with.

**One thing to watch:** P02's Q1 category (cigarettes) sits outside the catalogue the MVP models and
outside most category taxonomies. It is not codeable, but it is honest, and it says something about
what "available late at night" is doing for this user that a grocery-framed taxonomy would miss.

## Coding against `engine/schema.py`

| Field | Value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| `habit_driver` | *(no applicable value)* — nearest `satisficing` | see second-gap note below |
| `barrier` | *(no applicable value)* | same gap as P01 — "category is irrelevant" is not in the enum |
| `discovery_channel` | `search` | Q1 and Q4 both describe arriving with the item decided |
| `information_gap` | `price_benchmark` (pricing), `freshness_expiry` (quality) | Q3, verbatim order |
| `segment_signal` | — | explicitly not a parent |
| `habit_signal` | 1 | self-described regular shopper — recorded as stated, see note above |
| `pain_statement` | Orders frequently but always for a decided item; new categories are reached only when an urgent need has no nearby alternative. | Q1 + Q4 + Q5 |

`habit_signal` is coded 1 on P02's own words even though my reading of Q1/Q4 suggests fetch
behaviour. Coding the self-report rather than my interpretation keeps the disagreement visible in the
data instead of hiding it in a judgement call.

**A second enum gap, and it also replicates across both respondents.** P02's stated reason for
using the app at all is *availability when nothing else is open*: "it's late night and it is
available on zepto" (Q1), "no shop around me that time" (Q4). P01's Q1 is structurally identical —
"I'm at my hometown and only Blinkit is available there." In both cases the driver is the **absence
of an alternative**, not any of the six `HabitDriver` values, all of which describe a preference
between available options (`time_pressure`, `trust`, `satisficing`, `list_reuse`,
`price_certainty`, `household_routine`). I have recorded the nearest fit as `satisficing` but it is
a poor one: satisficing means settling for good enough, and neither respondent was choosing.

This matters beyond bookkeeping. If access-of-last-resort is a common driver, then a meaningful
share of orders happen in a context where the user has *no* alternative and *no* browsing intent —
the worst possible moment to surface a new category, and the moment the app is most likely to be
open. Two of two is not enough to claim that, but it is enough to record the hypothesis and check
it against the extraction output once the corpus finishes.
