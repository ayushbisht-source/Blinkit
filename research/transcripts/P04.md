## Analyst notes

### 1. The recruitment confound, tested — and the finding survives, narrowed

P01, P02 and P03 each named a never-bought category and explained it as irrelevance rather than
obstruction. Two of the three named **baby care**, both because they had no children. The obvious
worry, stated in the docs before this interview existed, was that this said more about a sample of
non-parents than about the instrument.

P04 has a newborn. Baby care is not irrelevant to them at all — **they buy it, and they buy it on a
quick-commerce app.** Pampers appears in both Q1 and Q4.

That is the correct outcome for the earlier finding: "this category does not apply to me" tracks the
respondent's **life stage**, exactly as claimed, rather than being an artifact of the question's
wording. A parent asked the same question does not produce the same answer.

**But it narrows the finding, and the narrowing has to be stated.** P04's never-bought category is
groceries, and their reason is a real barrier, not an absence of need. So across five respondents
the "irrelevance" pattern is **3 of 5**, not the 3 of 3 that the n=3 write-up reported. It remains a
genuine gap in `barrier[]` — three people gave honest answers with no valid code — but it is no
longer every respondent, and any claim that leaned on universality has to come down.

### 2. The first clean `channel_loyalty` case in the interviews

> *"there is one kirana store near me and we are buying from there like from ages"*

This is the one existing enum value that fits a respondent's answer exactly, with nothing left over.
`channel_loyalty` — "buys that category elsewhere by habit" — is precisely it. Worth noting because
the interviews have so far mostly produced answers the taxonomy could not hold; this one it holds
cleanly, which is evidence the enum is incomplete rather than wrong.

### 3. Q3 is the most consequential answer in the study so far

> *"Maybe when we shift to our new home or maybe when the kirana store which is near to our home
> will close permanently."*

The question asks **what information or evidence would unblock the category**. P04's answer contains
no information at all. Both unblocking conditions are **structural changes to their circumstances**:
move house, or the incumbent shuts down.

`docs/03-problem-definition.md` §2 locates the binding constraint in Fogg *ability* — can the user
evaluate an unfamiliar category fast enough inside a two-minute session. **For P04 that model does
not apply.** They are not failing to evaluate groceries on the app. They have a working supplier,
the relationship predates the app, and no amount of price anchoring, freshness evidence or trust
signalling changes that. The MVP's entire mechanism — supply the missing reference point — is aimed
at a constraint this respondent does not have.

This is the second respondent to describe a barrier that is not informational (P03's basket
economics was the first, and its mechanism was ambiguous; this one is not). Recorded as a genuine
challenge to the root cause, not a footnote.

### 4. Q4 replicates the trigger pattern, with the strongest instance yet

> *"Pampers because it's midnight all shops closed and we need that urgent for our baby."*

Urgency, plus total absence of an alternative, plus a need that cannot be deferred. That is the same
structure as P01 (hometown, only app available), P02 (late night, no shop nearby) and P03 (gym
joined, needed a sipper) — and it is the third respondent of five to describe **access of last
resort** as the reason the app got used at all.

Note the tension with Q1: the *last* order was also Pampers, but driven by **discounts**, not
urgency. So for the same user and the same category, one purchase was need-forced and the next was
price-led. Price appears here as what sustains a category once urgency has opened it — which is a
different and more useful claim than "price is the trigger."
