## Analyst notes

### 1. Third replication of "the category is irrelevant, not blocked"

P01: no kids, no pets. P02: no kids. P03: **lives alone, fully furnished — "nothing to do with it."**

Three of three respondents, on the question designed to find a *barrier*, have answered with an
*absence of need*, and none has named a competing channel because none is buying the category
anywhere. The `barrier[]` enum still has no code for this. At n=3 with 3 hits and 0 misses, this
stops being an anecdote about one person and starts being a property of the instrument.

Q3 replicates too, and identically to P01 and P02: *"when I have my own house I would love to buy"* —
against P01's *"if I have in future I would love to try"* and P02's *"in future I can try."* Three
for three on **dormant-pending-life-event** rather than rejected. And three for three on **quality**
as the named precondition (P03: *"quality matters to me"*).

### 2. Something genuinely new: the basket has a shape, and some categories don't fit it

The second half of P03's Q2 is the first thing any respondent has said that is not in this project's
vocabulary at all:

> *"we need to buy atleast minimum rs product like 150 or 200 rs of products for not to charge
> delivery fees too so I never bought home appliance"*

**The stated mechanism is ambiguous and I am not going to pretend otherwise.** A minimum-order
threshold should push a basket *up*, and a home appliance clears ₹200 easily, so read literally the
reason doesn't explain the behaviour. Three readings:

1. **Basket-shape mismatch.** The user has internalised what this app is *for* — assembling ₹150–200
   of small top-up items to clear free delivery. A home appliance isn't that kind of purchase, so it
   never enters consideration. The threshold isn't blocking the purchase; it has trained a habit
   about what belongs in the cart.
2. **Generalised fee grievance** that surfaced here because Q2 invited a complaint, and attaches to
   nothing in particular.
3. Something about the fee structure I haven't reconstructed correctly.

Reading 1 is the one I find most plausible and the most consequential, because it is a **different
kind of constraint from the one Part 3 is built on**. `docs/03-problem-definition.md` §2 locates the
binding constraint in Fogg *ability* — can the user evaluate an unfamiliar category fast enough
inside a two-minute session. Basket-shape mismatch is not about evaluation speed at all. The user
isn't failing to evaluate the home appliance; they never open the question, because the app is
mentally filed as somewhere you buy ₹150 of small things.

**This is exactly the follow-up an async text interview cannot do.** A live conversation would have
asked "what do you mean the minimum stops you — wouldn't an appliance be over ₹200 anyway?" and
resolved it in one exchange. It is recorded as ambiguous, and it is the clearest cost of the async
method in this project so far.

### 3. What the corpus says about it — the engine can see this and has nowhere to put it

Because this one *does* leave text, unlike the previous two gaps, it can be checked directly against
the 3,372-document corpus. Counted with `research/check_basket_economics.py`:

| | n |
|---|---:|
| Documents using minimum-order / delivery-fee / free-delivery-threshold language | **133** |
| — of which the relevance gate **kept** as relevant | 69 |
| — of which the relevance gate **rejected** | 64 (38 as `delivery`, 10 `unrelated`, 9 `support`, 5 `technical`, 2 `contentless`) |
| Narrower: language describing a **threshold shaping the basket** | **63** |
| — kept as relevant | 39 |

Two distinct problems, and they are worth separating:

**(a) The relevance gate discarded some of it.** Its instruction excludes "pure delivery-speed…
complaints", and a fee complaint reads like one. Among the rejects:

> *"the app is good but there is one problem that it is not free delivery tell me not at the items
> still 199 but there should be rs 100 that would be more suitable"* — rejected as `delivery`
>
> *"because free delivery on 149 not about 99 rs."* — rejected as `unrelated`

Those are people describing a threshold that governs what they put in a cart, filed as logistics
complaints.

**(b) More importantly, the ones it *kept* have nowhere to land.** None of the eight `barrier[]`
values describes basket economics. `price_risk` is defined as *"unsure if the price is fair"*, which
is a different claim — these users know exactly what the fee is; they are organising their basket
around it. Extraction will either force them into `price_risk`, misdescribing them, or drop the
signal.

> **Confirmed after extraction completed.** This paragraph originally ended "extraction will either
> force them into `price_risk` or drop the signal," written before the extraction stage had run.
> It did both. The threshold statements clustered into a theme of their own — **THM-05, 34
> statements, 5.4% of the corpus**, labelled `free / minimum / threshold / value` — and that theme's
> dominant barrier code is **`price_risk`**. The theme is real and the engine found it; the label
> attached to it says these users are unsure whether a price is fair, when what they actually said
> is that a threshold moved and they are re-planning their basket around it. Its representative
> quotes are unambiguous:
>
> *"399 ka lene par free delivery hai isko 99 rs par free delivery karna chahiye"* ·
> *"Before it serves free delivery on 100₹ , And now 😡😡 400₹"* ·
> *"delivery charge threshold should be at 100 rs not 130"* ·
> *"sudden increase in free delivery from ₹99 to ₹399 is unreasonable"*
>
> A prediction made from one interview, checked against 631 machine-extracted statements, and held.
> That is the cleanest demonstration in this project of what primary research is *for*: not more
> data, but knowing what the data means.

The strongest example the gate correctly kept:

> *"this scammer app.. when I adding 20/- item showing free delivery on above 150rs when I adding
> 180/- more item it's showing free delivery above 299/- and showing fake line"*

That is a user watching the threshold move as the basket grows and adjusting to it. There is no
field in `engine/schema.py` that can represent what they said.

**This gap differs in kind from the first two.** "Category is irrelevant" and access-of-last-resort
leave *no text at all* — no one writes a review about a product they have no reason to buy, so the
engine is structurally blind to them. Basket economics leaves plenty of text. The engine ingested
133 documents of it. The failure is in the **taxonomy**, not the collection: the vocabulary was
written before anyone asked a user an open question, and it has no slot for what they said.

### 4. The one thing that complicates the price story

P03's Q1 is the first order in this study **triggered by price**, and it involved deliberate
cross-platform comparison: *"good pricing 200 rs discount apart from other platforms."*

That cuts against the ordering P02 gave, where price arrived third as a confirmer after urgency and
absence of alternatives. Here price is the trigger and there is no urgency at all. It is also the
first non-grocery, non-consumable purchase anyone has described — an electronics accessory, which is
precisely the long-tail category expansion this project exists to increase.

Worth noting what it does *not* overturn: P03 still arrived knowing the item. They compared price on
a decided product; they did not browse. F2 survives.

### 5. Shopping rhythm — now 2 of 3, and still nobody browses

> *"I shop regularly from these quick commerce so nothing left."*

That matches P02 (*"I shop mostly regularly"*) against P01 (*"I shop when I needed the products"*).
So the self-reported split is 2 regular / 1 need-driven.

In `research/transcripts/P02.md` I wrote that if a later respondent described *actually browsing*,
the fetch-mode model in `docs/03-problem-definition.md` §2 would be in trouble. **P03 did not.**
Their two described purchases are a decided adapter and an urgent sipper — both single-item, both
with the product known before the app opened. Two respondents now self-describe as "regular" while
describing pure fetch behaviour, which is the second instance of the same reconciliation rather
than a repeat of my own inference: **frequency is not browsing**, and frequent-and-narrow is the
segment definition in `docs/00-foundation.md`, not a counterexample to it.

Standing prediction, unchanged: one respondent describing genuine browsing overturns this.

### 6. Q4 is the cleanest confirmation in the study so far

> *"Sipper, because i joined gym and i don't have sipper so i bought urgently."*

`docs/03-problem-definition.md` §5 claims crossover is event-triggered, and names the triggers:
*"ran out, a discount, a life event."* P03's crossover is a **life event** (joining a gym) creating
a need that did not previously exist, executed urgently, in a category they had never bought.

All three respondents now: P01 refined oil (ran out, urgent), P02 liquid detergent (urgent, no shop
nearby), P03 sipper (life event, urgent). Three for three, three different triggers, and every one
of them event-driven rather than curiosity-driven.

## Against the engine, survey, and P01–P02

| Claim | P03 |
|---|---|
| Barriers block latent demand (survey's framing) | **Challenged** — third replication; home appliance has no latent demand to block |
| Crossover is event-triggered, not curiosity-driven (`docs/03` §5) | **Confirmed, strongest instance** — a life event created the need |
| Users arrive knowing what they want (F2) | **Confirmed** — decided item in both Q1 and Q4, though Q1 adds cross-platform price comparison |
| Information needed before trying includes quality (F3) | **Confirmed** — third respondent to name it unprompted |
| Price is a confirmer, not a trigger (P02's ordering) | **Challenged** — here price *is* the trigger, with no urgency present |
| Shopping is need-triggered, not routine (P01's Q5) | **Partially challenged** — self-describes as regular, but describes fetch behaviour; second instance of the same pattern as P02 |
| The binding constraint is evaluation speed (Fogg ability, `docs/03` §2) | **Possibly challenged** — basket-shape mismatch would be a constraint of a different kind, upstream of evaluation. n=1, mechanism ambiguous. |
| The engine's vocabulary is adequate to the problem | **Challenged, with corpus evidence** — 133 documents of basket-economics language and no field to record it in |

**Caveat:** n=3, convenience sample. All three respondents are non-parents; two live alone or
describe single-person households. The repeated "category is irrelevant" finding may partly reflect
who was available to ask — that is a real confound and it is not resolved by three interviews. What
the three *do* establish is that the taxonomy is incomplete, which does not require a representative
sample to demonstrate.
