# Synthetic Personas — AI-generated from survey data

> ## ⚠️ THESE ARE NOT INTERVIEWS
>
> **No person said any of these words.** Each persona below is an LLM-generated narrative built
> around one real respondent's actual survey answers. The *selections* are real — app, order mix,
> stated blockers, household type, near-miss reason — and are printed verbatim under each persona
> so any claim can be checked against source data. The *dialogue* is generated.
>
> Names are labels for readability, not people. Do not cite these as participants, respondents or
> interviewees. They are excluded from the evidence base in `docs/02-user-research.md` and are not
> cited in Part 3.
>
> **What they are for:** hypothesis generation, and a demonstration of what AI-synthesized research
> can and cannot do. See "What this exercise proved" at the end — that section is the actual
> finding.

Source: 40-response survey, 28–29 July 2026. Row numbers refer to submission order.

Five respondents were chosen to span the meaningful variation in the data rather than at random:
a light user who already crossed into pet supplies, a heavy user citing every barrier at once, a
groceries-only user whose answers contradict each other, a user whose intent evaporates mid-cart,
and a kirana-loyal user who defaults offline.

## P1 — "Ananya", 24 · synthesized from **Row 20**

> **Real survey selections (verbatim):** Blinkit · 1–2 orders/month · chose it for fast delivery ·
> orders Groceries, Snacks & beverages, Household essentials, Personal care, **Pet supplies** ·
> "Mostly know" what she wants · bulk from quick-commerce app · wanted to try something new
> "Sometimes" · held back because **"Took too much time to explore/compare options"** · **has**
> bought a new category in last 3 months · reason for not trying more: **"Just habit — buy the same
> things"** · goes to Swiggy Instamart first for something new · last near-miss: **"Too expensive"** ·
> lives with roommates

**Q1 — Last order, and what made you open the app?**
Dog food, mostly. And bread and some chips because I was already there. I opened it because we ran
out of dog food that morning — that was the actual reason, the rest just got added.

**Q2 — A category you've never bought from?**
Baby care, home stuff, anything like that. I don't need baby things. Home stuff I'd just get from
the supermarket when I'm there anyway.

**Q3 — What would have to be true to buy it on the app?**
Honestly the problem is it takes too long to figure out. If I have to open three things and compare,
I'm done — I'll just finish my order. It would have to be obvious in about five seconds.

**Q4 — Last time you bought something new on the app?**
The pet supplies, but that wasn't really me exploring. My dog needed food, the shop was shut, Blinkit
had the brand I already use. So I bought what I already trusted, just faster.

**Q5 — Anything missed?**
I order maybe twice a month, so when I do order I'm buying everything at once. It probably looks
like I try lots of categories, but it's the opposite — I'm just doing one big order instead of five
small ones.

**Generated hypothesis:** category breadth can reflect *order consolidation* rather than curiosity.
Infrequent users bundle. → **Checkable against real data — see below.**

## P2 — "Rohit", 27 · synthesized from **Row 17**

> **Real survey selections (verbatim):** Blinkit · 10+ orders/month · fast delivery · orders
> Groceries, Snacks & beverages, Household essentials, Personal care, **Baby products** · "Mostly
> know" · bulk from quick-commerce app · wanted to try but skipped **"Yes, often"** · held back:

**Q1 — Last order?**
Milk, bread, detergent, some snacks. Nothing interesting. I order ten-plus times a month so it's
basically restocking on autopilot.

**Q2 — Never bought from?**
Pet supplies, anything home or kitchen. I don't have a pet. Kitchen stuff I'd want to actually see.

**Q3 — What would have to be true?**
Depends entirely on the category, which is maybe the annoying part. For food it's taste risk. For
personal care it's whether I trust the brand. For anything expensive it's just price. It's not one
thing I need — it's a different thing each time.

**Q4 — Last new thing?**
Baby products, for my sister's kid. I knew exactly what to get because she told me. So not really
discovery — someone else did the deciding.

**Q5 — Anything missed?**
I said I want reviews *and* I don't trust brands *and* it's habit *and* price. All of those are
true, just for different things. One reason per person is probably the wrong model.

**Generated hypothesis:** barriers are category-specific rather than user-specific — the same person
carries different blockers for different categories.

## P3 — "Divya", 29 · synthesized from **Row 33**

**Q1 — Last order?**
Atta, dal, oil, vegetables. Same as most weeks. I know what's finished before I open the app.

**Q2 — Never bought from?**

**Q3 — What would have to be true?**
With food and groceries, if something isn't right, that's money wasted and it's my family eating it.
I'm not experimenting with that on a maybe.

**Q4 — But you did buy something new recently?**

## Testing the generated hypotheses against real data

The point of a hypothesis is that it can be checked. P1's is the only one that is directly
computable from the survey — so it was computed.

**P1's claim:** breadth reflects consolidation, so *less frequent* users should show *more*
categories.

| Order frequency | Respondents | Mean categories ordered |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1–2 / month | 8 | 2.75 |
| 3–5 / month | 11 | 2.64 |
| 6–10 / month | 9 | 3.22 |
| 10+ / month | 12 | 3.42 |

Pearson *r* between frequency rank and category count: **+0.25**.

**The hypothesis is not supported.** It predicts a *negative* relationship — infrequent users
bundling more categories per order. The observed relationship is weakly *positive*: heavier users
have slightly broader baskets. The pattern is also not clean (3–5/month sits marginally below
1–2/month), and at n=40 an *r* of 0.25 is suggestive at best. The honest summary is that
consolidation is not what produces breadth here, and the correlation is too weak to claim much
beyond that.

That result matters more than any of the personas. A fluent, plausible narrative produced a claim
the data does not support — and it took a two-line calculation to find out.

> **A correction worth recording.** The first version of this table was wrong: the counts and means
> printed here were generated alongside the personas rather than computed, and differed materially
> from the real figures (6/9/9/10 and 3.0/3.2/3.6/3.9 against the actual 8/11/9/12 and
> 2.75/2.64/3.22/3.42). The directional conclusion survived; the numbers did not. It is an
> uncomfortably exact illustration of this document's own argument — plausible-sounding output is
> not evidence until something checks it — and the numbers above are now computed from the raw rows
> by `research/verify_personas.py`.

## What this exercise proved

The personas read convincingly. Four of the five hypotheses they produced are untestable from the
survey, and the one that *was* testable turned out to be false.

That is not a flaw in the generation. It is the structural limit:

> **A persona generated from survey responses cannot contain information the survey did not capture.
> It can recombine and articulate what is already there — fluently enough to feel like insight — but
> it cannot surprise, and it has no mechanism for being right about anything outside its input.**

This is why the brief's framing — *"AI-generated insights are only a starting point"* — is correct,
and it is why real interviews are not substitutable. A real person answering Q5 ("anything I've
completely missed?") can introduce a barrier nobody thought to ask about. A persona built from a
fixed-option survey structurally cannot: its entire vocabulary is the survey's vocabulary.

**Recorded as a methodological finding of this project, and as the reason `research/async-interview-kit.md`
exists.**
